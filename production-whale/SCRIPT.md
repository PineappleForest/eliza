## Facts (with sources)
- First picked up in 1989 by US Navy hydrophones (SOSUS) set up in the Cold War to track Soviet submarines; at first
  thought to be mechanical. Scientific American: https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/inside-the-nail-biting-quest-to-find-the-loneliest-whale/
- The call sits at ~52 Hz; blue whales call around 10-39 Hz (usually 15-20), fin whales ~20 Hz.
  Smithsonian: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/maybe-worlds-loneliest-whale-isnt-so-isolated-after-all-180955005/
- Watkins and colleagues at Woods Hole tracked and recorded the calls 1992-2004 (paper: "Twelve years of tracking 52-Hz
  whale calls from a unique source in the North Pacific", Watkins et al. 2004). Only one series of 52-Hz calls was
  recorded at a time, with no overlap (Discover Wildlife: https://discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/marine-animals/52-hertz-whale).
- No one has ever seen the whale (Discover Wildlife; Scientific American).
- Nicknamed "the loneliest whale in the world".
- Caveat kept out of the script's claims: other whales CAN hear 52 Hz (Christopher Clark, Cornell); the script says no one
  "sings back" / no second voice like it, not that whales can't hear it. Calls with a similar pattern were picked up at
  more than one place in 2010 (possible others).
